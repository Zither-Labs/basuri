The murali (end-blown)

Here murali means the bamboo flute you hold more like a Western recorder: you blow into one end of the tube (often through a duct / whistle mouthpiece), not across a hole on the side.

That is a different instrument family from the basuri / bansuri this room mainly teaches — the side-blown flute where air goes across an open embouchure hole on the wall of the bamboo.

Murali vs basuri — at a glance

Murali (this page)Basuri / bansuri
How you blow Into the end of the bamboo (duct / fipple, recorder-like) Across a side hole with shaped lips
Hold More vertical / end-on Horizontal (transverse)
First sound Usually easier — the mouthpiece aims the air for you Harder — you must find the embouchure edge yourself
Tone control Less bend / meend flexibility More pitch and colour control (classical preference)
Where you hear it Folk, toys, film, children’s flutes Hindustani / Nepali classical and serious practice

How you blow an end-blown murali

  1. Seal your lips around the mouthpiece end (or the carved duct opening) — similar to a recorder.
  2. Blow a steady stream into the tube. A built-in edge (fipple) splits the air and makes the tone.
  3. Cover finger holes to change pitch. Soft, even air; do not blast.

Because the instrument aims the air for you, many people get a tone on day one — which is why these flutes are common as toys and folk instruments. The trade-off: you cannot shape the airstream as freely as on a side-blown basuri, so classical slides and fine intonation are harder.

Shop labels are messy. Some sellers call any bamboo flute “murali.” Look at the mouthpiece: if you blow into the tip like a recorder, it is the end-blown type on this page; if there is an open hole on the side of the tube and you hold it sideways, it is a basuri-style flute.

Komal & tivra on murali

Same music theory as on basuri (full chart on Swaras → Komal & tivra):

  • Komal Re, Ga, Dha, Ni — flatten by half-holing the hole that sits between two full notes.
  • Tivra Ma — the raised Ma; usually the fully open (or next full) fingering on your tube.
  • Shuddha Ma — half-cover between Ga and that open Ma.
  • Sa and Pa stay fixed.

What differs on the end-blown murali:

  1. Find two neighbouring full notes (e.g. Sa and Re). Leave a thin crack on the hole that opens between them until the pitch sits in the middle against the drone.
  2. Hold that crack steady — do not use hard breath to “force” the komal; pressure tends to sharpen or squeak.
  3. For tivra Ma, use the clear full open note; do not half-hole that one.

Folk muralis often do not follow the classical “Sa = top three closed” spacing. If your holes feel wrong for that chart, pick any steady tonic as Sa, then half-hole between successive open/closed pairs by ear. The drone still decides “in tune.”

Basuri players can also bend with embouchure and meend; murali players lean harder on accurate half-cover. Prefer the side-blown path if you want raga-ready komal / tivra control.

Names, Krishna, and confusion

In poetry and bhajans, murali is also Krishna’s flute — and that image is often the side-blown bansuri in paintings. Folk speech in South Asia may use murli / murali / bansuri for different tubes. This page uses murali for the end-blown, recorder-like bamboo flute so beginners are not sent to the wrong embouchure.

Instrument writers (e.g. chandrakantha.com) split bamboo flutes the same way: transverse (side-blown, preferred for classical) vs fipple (duct / whistle, folk and children’s instruments).

Which should you learn?

  • Want classical bansuri / this room’s path — buy a side-blown basuri and start at first sound (straw, kiss-and-roll across the side hole).
  • Want an easy first noise or a folk/toy flute — an end-blown murali is fine; enjoy it, but know the blowing skill does not fully transfer to basuri embouchure.
  • Have both — use the murali for fun tunes; practice long tones and Sa on the basuri if that is your real goal.
